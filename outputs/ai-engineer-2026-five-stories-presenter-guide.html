The retrieved values then need to retain their meaning. Ganesh describes typed facts carrying value, unit, scale, and period, with registered anchors back to the supporting material. The important transition is from extracting a value to maintaining an inspectable relationship between the claim, its meaning, and its evidence. The final generation step still needs a check.

Row-count validation cannot detect entity and aggregation errors

Discussion focusRead the requested scope, then the inputs and validation tables. Explain why shape agreement passes while the business answer fails.

Everything in this example is invented. The user asks for advisor A17’s net flow in the third quarter. Our teaching definition is purchases minus redemptions. Purchases are eight hundred thousand dollars. Redemptions are one million. The firm-wide total is positive five million dollars.

The candidate answer returns exactly one row and confidently says A17 had positive five million of net flow. It looks tidy. If we compare only the number of rows, it passes.

The failure is the source scope. Five million belongs to the firm aggregate, not the advisor. The advisor calculation is eight hundred thousand minus one million, giving negative two hundred thousand dollars. The source can be real, the calculation can be valid at one scope, and the answer can still be wrong for this user’s question.

This is our application of Kepler’s lesson. We should be able to inspect the entity, measure, period, units, keyed values, and calculation behind the final claim. An LLM judgment may help with semantics, but it needs examples adjudicated by people who know the business.

Even the correct negative figure does not establish that an advisor is dissatisfied or moving assets to a competitor. That would be another unsupported leap. The number needs context, and the narrative needs its own evidence. That brings us to Bridgewater’s story about which context an answer receives.
